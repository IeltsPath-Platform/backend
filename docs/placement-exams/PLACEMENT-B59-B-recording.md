**Assistant:** Good morning, Oakwood Cookery School. How can I help?

**Caller:** Hello. I saw your advertisement for the Italian cooking course and I'd like to find out more.

**Assistant:** Certainly. It's a six-week course, and the classes are on Thursday evenings.

**Caller:** What time do they start?

**Assistant:** Seven o'clock, and they finish at half past nine. The next course begins on the twenty-second of January.

**Caller:** Good. And what will we learn?

**Assistant:** In the first week we make fresh pasta, and in the second week, sauces. After that we cover bread, then desserts, and in week five it's a whole menu for dinner guests. In the final week you cook a three-course meal for the other students.

**Caller:** That sounds like a lot of fun. Do I have to bring anything?

**Assistant:** Just an apron. Everything else, including the ingredients, is provided.

**Caller:** How much is the course?

**Assistant:** It's two hundred and ten pounds for the six weeks, but if you book before the end of December there's a discount of fifteen per cent.

**Caller:** Excellent. How many people are in each class?

**Assistant:** We limit it to twelve, so that everyone gets attention from the chef.

**Caller:** Are there any places left?

**Assistant:** Only three, I'm afraid, so I'd book soon. Could I take your details?

**Caller:** Of course. My name is Marcus Reyes, that's R, E, Y, E, S, and I live at 14 Mill Lane.

**Assistant:** Thank you, Mr Reyes. I'll send a confirmation today. The school is just opposite the library, ten minutes from the station.

**Speaker:** Good evening, everyone, and thank you for coming to the opening of the Millbrook Arts Centre. I'd like to take a few minutes to tell you how the centre came about and what you can do here.

**Speaker:** The building was a primary school until 2009, when the number of children in the area fell and the school closed. For several years it stood empty, and the council planned to sell it to a property developer. However, a group of local residents formed a committee, raised money, and persuaded the council to lease it to them for a very low rent instead.

**Speaker:** It took us three years and a great deal of volunteer work to convert the building. Fortunately, we kept most of the original features. The old hall is now a theatre which seats a hundred and fifty, and the classrooms have become studios for painting, pottery and photography. The playground was turned into a garden, which will be used for outdoor concerts in summer.

**Speaker:** The centre will be open from ten in the morning until ten at night, Tuesday to Sunday, and closed on Mondays for cleaning and repairs. Most of our evening courses cost between eight and twelve pounds a session, but anyone under eighteen can attend free of charge, thanks to a grant from a local company.

**Speaker:** We're always looking for volunteers. At present we particularly need people to help in the café and to look after the box office on theatre nights. You don't need any experience; we'll give you training. If you're interested, please speak to Jenny at the desk by the entrance, or leave your name on the form.

**Speaker:** Finally, I'd like to thank everyone who gave money, time or both. The centre belongs to the community, and we hope you'll all make it your own.

## Listening Part 3: a project on sleep and memory

**Tutor:** Come in, Priya and Jack. Let's talk about your research project on sleep and memory. How far have you got?

**Priya:** We've finished the experiment. We asked forty students to learn a list of sixty word pairs in the evening. Half of them then slept normally, and the other half stayed awake all night.

**Jack:** We tested all of them again two days later, so that tiredness wouldn't affect the results.

**Tutor:** That was sensible. What did you find?

**Priya:** The students who slept remembered about twenty per cent more word pairs. We expected a difference, but not as big as that.

**Jack:** What surprised me was that the effect was larger for the harder pairs, the ones with no obvious connection. For easy pairs like salt and pepper, both groups did well.

**Tutor:** That fits with other research. Did you check whether the students who stayed awake were less motivated?

**Priya:** We thought about it, but all the participants were paid, so we assumed that motivation was the same.

**Tutor:** Hmm. Payment doesn't guarantee that. You could mention it as a limitation. What about the sample? Forty is a small group.

**Jack:** Yes, and they were all university students, so we can't say that the results apply to older people. We'll say that in the discussion section.

**Tutor:** Good. How are you planning to present the results?

**Priya:** I'd like to use a bar chart comparing the two groups, because it's clear and simple.

**Jack:** I think we should include a table with the exact figures too, in an appendix.

**Tutor:** Both are fine. Now, who's writing the literature review?

**Priya:** I'll do that, because I've already read most of the papers.

**Jack:** And I'll do the statistics. I'm more confident with that program.

**Priya:** We'll write the introduction together, and Jack will draft the conclusion.

**Tutor:** What about the abstract? Remember it needs to be under two hundred words.

**Priya:** I'll write that last.

**Tutor:** Excellent. The deadline is the fifteenth of May, so you have three weeks. Come and see me again after you've written a first draft.

## Listening Part 4: the history of paper

- File: `part4.mp3`, key `listening/placement/placement-b59-b/part4.mp3`

- Tên người nói in đậm chỉ để biết ai nói, **không đọc ra**. Không đọc số part hay hướng dẫn câu hỏi.

**Lecturer:** Today I want to look at the history of paper, a material so common that we rarely think about it.

**Lecturer:** Before paper, people wrote on a variety of materials. In Egypt they used papyrus, made from a reed that grew along the River Nile. Strips of the plant were pressed together to make a flat sheet. In Europe, parchment, made from animal skin, was the main material, but it was expensive, because a single book might need the skins of dozens of sheep.

**Lecturer:** Paper as we know it was developed in China. Traditionally, the invention is credited to an official named Cai Lun, who presented the process to the emperor in the year 105. Early Chinese paper was made from old rags, tree bark and fishing nets, which were beaten into a pulp, spread on a screen and left to dry.

**Lecturer:** For several centuries the method stayed a secret. It reached the Arab world in the eighth century, and the first paper mills in Europe were built in Spain in the twelfth century. After that, paper production spread slowly across the continent.

**Lecturer:** The turning point came in the fifteenth century, when Johannes Gutenberg developed printing with movable metal type. Suddenly there was a huge demand for cheap paper, and for the first time a shortage of rags became a serious problem.

**Lecturer:** The solution arrived in the 1840s, when a German inventor, Friedrich Keller, found a way to make paper from wood pulp. Wood was far cheaper and more plentiful than rags, and paper became cheap enough for newspapers and inexpensive books to reach ordinary readers. However, early wood paper had a disadvantage: it contained acid, so it turned yellow and fell apart after a few decades.

**Lecturer:** Today most paper is still made from wood, but recycling has become important, and a great deal of paper is now made from used paper, which saves both trees and energy.
