**Lecturer:** Before paper, people wrote on a variety of materials. In Egypt they used papyrus, made from a reed that grew along the River Nile. **Lecturer:** For several centuries the method stayed a secret. It reached the Arab world in the eighth century, and the first paper mills in Europe were built in Spain in the twelfth century. After that, paper production spread slowly across the continent.

**Lecturer:** The turning point came in the fifteenth century, when Johannes Gutenberg developed printing with movable metal type. Suddenly there was a huge demand for cheap paper, and for the first time a shortage of rags became a serious problem.

**Lecturer:** The solution arrived in the 1840s, when a German inventor, Friedrich Keller, found a way to make paper from wood pulp. Wood was far cheaper and more plentiful than rags, and paper became cheap enough for newspapers and inexpensive books to reach ordinary readers. However, early wood paper had a disadvantage: it contained acid, so it turned yellow and fell apart after a few decades.

**Lecturer:** Today most paper is still made from wood, but recycling has become important, and a great deal of paper is now made from used paper, which saves both trees and energy.

The Return of the Beaver

For about four hundred years there were no wild beavers in Britain. Hunted for their fur and for a substance used in perfume and medicine, the animals had disappeared from the island by the sixteenth century. In recent years, however, they have returned. A few families were released in carefully controlled trials, and conservationists have since watched with great interest as the animals have gone about the work for which they are famous: building dams.

A beaver dam is made of sticks, branches and mud. Its purpose is to create a pond deep enough for the animal to build a safe home, called a lodge, with an entrance below the water. The ponds that result have effects far beyond the beavers' own needs. Water that would otherwise run quickly down a slope is held back and released slowly, so that the stream below is less likely to flood after heavy rain. In dry weather the pond works in the opposite way, because it keeps a supply of water in the landscape for much longer.

The changes do not stop there. The ponds trap mud that would otherwise be carried downstream, which makes the water cleaner for the fish and insects that live further along. Trees that beavers cut down often grow back in a new shape, with many thin stems, and this gives shelter to small birds. Wetland plants arrive, then frogs, dragonflies and water voles. Ecologists describe the beaver as a 'keystone' species, meaning that one animal has an effect on the whole environment far greater than its size would suggest.

Not everyone is delighted, however. Farmers whose fields lie beside a stream worry that a new dam may flood their crops, and some anglers fear that dams will block the movement of fish such as salmon and trout. Research so far suggests that these fears are partly justified: in a few places land has been flooded and the owners have had to be compensated. On the other hand, studies of fish have usually found that dams slow, but do not stop, the movement of the larger species.

The practical response has been to manage rather than to prevent. In some places engineers fit a pipe through a dam so that the water level stays below a set height, which keeps the pond but protects the field. In others, fences are built around valuable trees. These measures are cheap compared with the cost of repairing flood damage elsewhere, and where they have been used, many landowners have become more positive.

Supporters point out that the beaver does for free what humans would otherwise pay a great deal to do. Building artificial wetlands to slow rivers can cost millions, whereas a family of beavers will build several in a few seasons. Whether the country is ready to live with such an energetic neighbour is a question that politicians, farmers and ordinary residents are still debating.

The Economics of Coffee

A. Coffee is one of the most widely traded agricultural products in the world. Every day, people drink billions of cups, yet most of them never think about the long journey the beans have made. They are grown in about seventy tropical countries, often by farmers who own only a few hectares, and then roasted and sold thousands of kilometres away.

B. The price of coffee is set on international markets and changes constantly. When a frost or a drought damages the harvest in a major producing country, the price can double within months; when there is a very large harvest, it can fall just as quickly. For farmers, this makes planning almost impossible. A family may earn a good living one year and barely cover its costs the next.

C. Most of the money in the coffee chain is made far from the farm. Of the price a customer pays in a cafe in Europe or North America, only a small share goes to the farmer. Much of the rest pays for transport, roasting, packaging, rent and wages, and for the profits of the large companies that buy the beans. As a result, many growers cannot afford to improve their land or to send their children to school.

D. In response, some shoppers have turned to products with 'fair trade' labels, which guarantee a minimum price to producers. Supporters argue that the system gives farmers security and encourages good environmental practice. Critics reply that the extra money paid by consumers often does not reach the poorest workers, who are employed by farmers rather than owning land, and that certification costs can be too high for the smallest growers.

E. Another approach is to remove the middle steps. Some cooperatives of farmers now roast and pack their own coffee and sell it directly to cafes abroad. This brings in a much higher price per kilo, but it demands skills and equipment that many small producers lack. Training programmes, often paid for by charities and governments, have helped some groups to succeed, though they remain a minority.

F. Climate change may soon make all of these questions more urgent. Coffee plants grow well only within a narrow range of temperatures, and researchers expect a large part of today's growing areas to become unsuitable over the coming decades. Farmers could move to higher ground, where it is cooler, but there is limited land there, and much of it is protected forest. The future of the world's favourite drink may therefore depend on finding plants that tolerate heat.

## Reading Passage 3: does money buy happiness?

_Questions 27–31: Choose the correct letter, A, B, C or D. Questions 32–36: Do the statements agree with the views of the writer? Choose YES, NO or NOT GIVEN. Questions 37–40: Complete the sentences. Choose ONE WORD ONLY from the passage. You should spend about 20 minutes on this passage._

Does Money Buy Happiness?

A. For decades, newspapers have reported that money cannot buy happiness. The claim is repeated so often that it seems obvious, yet researchers who have tried to test it have reached conclusions that are neither simple nor consistent. The most famous early study, published in 2010, analysed the answers of hundreds of thousands of people and reported that happiness in daily life rose with income only up to a certain level, after which more money made little difference. The finding was quoted by writers, politicians and advertisers alike.

B. More recent work has questioned it. When a team of economists examined larger and more recent sets of data, they found that, on average, happiness continued to rise at every level of income they measured, with no sign of a limit. Other researchers then re-examined the same figures and suggested that both views were partly correct. Most people did seem to become happier as they earned more, but for a minority who were already unhappy, extra money made no difference at all. It was the average that hid the difference.

C. An important question is what 'happiness' means. Psychologists distinguish between emotional wellbeing, the day-to-day feelings of joy or worry, and life evaluation, a person's overall judgement of how well their life is going. Income appears to have a stronger link with the second than with the first. A higher salary may not make a person smile more on an ordinary Tuesday, but it often makes them more satisfied when they think about their life as a whole, since it brings security and choice.

D. Why should this be? One explanation is that money buys freedom from particular worries: rent, medical bills, an unreliable car. For people on low incomes, a small rise can remove a real source of stress, and its effect is large. For those who are already comfortable, extra money tends to be spent on things they will quickly get used to, so the effect is small. Economists call this 'diminishing returns'. Another explanation lies in comparison. People often judge their income not in isolation but against that of others, and a pay rise matters less if colleagues have received the same.

E. It would be wrong, however, to conclude that wealth is the main route to a good life. Studies of what people say makes them happiest usually put relationships first, followed by health and a sense of purpose. Those who spend money on shared experiences, or on other people, are generally happier than those who buy things for themselves. In one experiment, volunteers were given a small sum and told to spend it either on themselves or on someone else; those who gave it away reported feeling better at the end of the day.

F. The practical lesson for governments is therefore double-edged. Policies that raise the incomes of the poorest, such as a higher minimum wage, seem likely to produce real gains in wellbeing. At the same time, there is little evidence that making already wealthy societies richer will, on its own, make them happier. Whether a country should aim for faster growth or a better balance of work and leisure is not a question that the data alone can answer.
